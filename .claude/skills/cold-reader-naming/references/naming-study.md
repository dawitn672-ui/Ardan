# How good works make names a newcomer can read cold (the saved study)

*Stamped 2026-11-27 ET. Made for the owner's order 97 ("study naming in historical and fantasy works; every name
understandable to a newcomer ... easy to learn, long to master"), order 100 (the cold-reader naming conventions are
ours to set) and the owner's addition of the same day: "cold reader naming needs to far exceed what currently exists in
the game, using plain language a lot of the time instead of random buzz words like wyrm." The owner's original words
(docs/prompts/2026-11-26-world-rebuild-and-diagnostic.md): "kesh becomes kingdom of kesh ... i wont know what a 7th
gate is so put its simple term with it same with a gatewarden the scorched march etc. a user shouldnt need to spend
hours just understanding the naming although it should take much longer than that to fully understand every part of
this world."*

Sources are listed at the end with the claim each supports. Where a source is a blog or a fan wiki, it says so; the
rules below rest on the pattern across many works, not on any one of them.

## 1. History: real names were plain words once

- **English place names are descriptions that wore down.** Oxford is Old English *Oxnaford*, "the ford where oxen
  cross"; Newcastle upon Tyne is a new castle (built about 1080) on the Tyne, and is told apart from Newcastle-under-Lyme
  by the second half, a plain locator [1][2]. The elements are a small set of common nouns: ford, bridge, castle,
  ham/ton (farm, settlement), wick (trading place), mere (lake), dale, burn. A medieval traveller understood them; a
  modern reader still half-understands them (Saltbridge, Kingsmere, Northmoor). **Lesson:** a made-up place built from
  plain English parts (Salt + bridge) is read cold; a made-up string (Zabesh) is not.
- **Titles name a rank and a domain.** "Duke of Normandy", "Earl of Wessex", "Bishop of Durham", "Captain of the
  Guard": the first word says what the person is, the rest says over what. A reader who has never heard of Wessex still
  knows an earl rules land and stands below a king.
- **Bynames are plain words about a deed or a look.** William the Conqueror, Edward Longshanks (he was tall), John
  Lackland (a youngest son with no land to inherit), Richard the Lionheart [3]. One byname a person, earned, in words a
  child knows. The epithet is a story in two words, not a sound.

## 2. Fantasy: the works people remember translate their names

- **Tolkien translated on purpose.** In *The Lord of the Rings* the Common Speech (Westron) names are given in English
  "to preserve the effect to the English reader": Rivendell (riven + dell) for Imladris, Mount Doom for Orodruin
  ("mountain of fire"), the Shire, Bree, Crickhollow [4][5]. His *Guide to the Names* told translators to translate
  English-form names by their meaning [4]. The Elvish names stay as a second layer the curious reader digs into. That is
  "easy to learn, long to master" exactly: Mount Doom on the map, Orodruin in the lore.
- **A Song of Ice and Fire** keeps the names a reader must hold in their head plain and literal: the Wall (a wall),
  King's Landing (where the king landed), the Iron Throne (a throne of swords), the North, the Eyrie, the Red Keep. The
  invented names (Westeros, Targaryen) are few and carried by these plain neighbours [6]. Martin has said naming is hard
  and envied Jack Vance's gift for it [6].
- **Earthsea**: every person has a use-name (Sparrowhawk) and a true name (Ged) that is power [7]. The use-name is the
  plain one the world calls him; the invented word is rare and meaningful. Islands and towns are short and distinct.
- **Dark Souls** puts plain English in front of the strange: Firelink Shrine, the Undead Burg, Blighttown, the Painted
  World, Sen's Fortress; the invented names (Anor Londo, Lordran) are few and arrive once the player has a foothold [8].
- **The Witcher** keeps Elder Speech for the elves (Kaer Morhen, "old sea fortress"; Gwynbleidd, "white wolf") and gives
  the hero his plain name in the common speech: "the White Wolf", "Geralt of Rivia" [9]. A far people sounds different,
  but the reader always has the plain gloss.
- **The Elder Scrolls**: "the Imperial City", "the Imperial Province" sit beside elvish Cyrodiil [10]; Skyrim's towns are
  plain compounds (Whiterun, Riverwood, Windhelm).
- **Korean web novels and manhwa** (the owner's own reference): *Solo Leveling* ranks are letters (E to S), and the
  great titles are plain English words in the translation: Shadow Monarch, the Rulers, Hunter, Gate [11].
  *Overgeared*'s class is "Pagma's Successor" and its skills say what they do: "Legendary Blacksmith's Craftsmanship" [12].
  The pattern: rank words are a ladder anyone can order; titles are plain nouns with one proper name at most.

## 3. Readers and players: what the evidence says

- **A meaning is remembered; a label is not.** The Baker/baker effect: shown a face, people recall that the man is a
  baker far more often than that his name is Baker (one study: 82.5% occupations against 60% surnames), because the
  job hooks into what they know and the name hooks into nothing [13]. A name that *is* a description (the Iron Warden,
  the Salt Coast) borrows the job's advantage.
- **Similar items are confused and forgotten.** Lists of similar-sounding items are recalled worse, in item as well as
  order (the phonological similarity effect) [14]. Strategy players accepted invented unit names but complained when
  they had to tell a TU-34 from a TU-37 [15]. Corvasse, Corvane and Morvane in one world are a TU-34 and a TU-37.
- **What makes an invented word stick** is distinctiveness: in a study of 2,100 pseudowords, the memorable ones looked
  like rare real words, had few look-alike neighbours and unusual letter pairs [16] (the reading as an item property is
  debated [16]). **Lesson:** if a name must be invented, make it unlike its neighbours, short, and pronounceable.
- **Ration new words.** The "five finger fantasy rule": no more than three, at most four, world-specific words on a
  novel's first page, names included; once met, a word no longer counts as new [17] (a blogger's guideline, not
  research). The common writing advice is the same: a few foreign words give a sense of elsewhere, too many cannot be
  held [18][19]. In games: "Too many proper nouns and suddenly the game's focus is not on adventure and drama" [20].
- **Invent words only for what is unique to your world.** "Use invented vocabulary only for things that are unique to
  your world", names "should still hint at what something is", and do not rename what players already know [15]. A
  dragon is a dragon; "wyrm" renames a thing the player already knows.
- **Explain in place.** Crusader Kings III's nested tooltips (a highlighted term opens its definition, which links its
  own terms) are counted its most important accessibility feature [21]; Avowed adds each term to a glossary as it is
  spoken [22]. In Ardan a description opens on **click, never hover** (standing rule): `data-note="..."`.
- **Keep the voice consistent.** A naming style that wanders (keyboard-smashed names beside English ones) is what
  readers complain of [18][23]; giving each culture a recognisable sound pattern helps [23].

## 4. What this study makes the rules (the skill's rules, in short)

1. Plain English is the default for everything a player reads; an invented or archaic word must earn its place.
2. Every proper name meets the player with its descriptor: the Kingdom of Kesh, Ashmere (the lake kingdom), Morvane, a
   demon lord.
3. A name says what it is or where it is (Saltbridge, the Wall, Mount Doom); numbers and ordinals say of what.
4. One invented word per name at most; three new names per screen at most.
5. Titles are rank + domain in plain words; one word means one thing.
6. Names that stand together look and sound different (first letter, length, ending).
7. Far and non-human peoples may sound different, always with the plain gloss beside, and a plain English name the
   humans use.
8. Depth lives in a second layer (the lore name, the history) the player opens by choice, never in the label.
